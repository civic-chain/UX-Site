# Benefits by Audience

## What This Document Owns

This is the positive business reference for **who benefits and why**. Use it
before rewriting landing strategy, pitch copy, stakeholder summaries, or sales
slides.

It translates product facts, evidence, savings mechanics, dollar modeling, and
journeys into audience-specific value. Facts still stay in
[[_docs/facts/product]] and evidence memos; detailed flows stay in journey docs;
the savings mechanism stays in [[_docs/business/3-model/customer-savings-mechanism]];
planning dollars stay in [[_docs/business/3-model/customer-savings-dollar-model]].

Narrative rule: every third-party win must fold back into the municipal buyer's
payoff. Resident transparency, vendor speed, and worker convenience are not
separate charity or public-good messages; they are proof that the town gets more
bidders, lower modeled risk premium, less staff time, stronger audit posture,
and a decision it can defend.

## Benefit Thesis

CivicChain gives small governments a way to pay vendors faster after approval,
control contract spend before it becomes a problem, and make every payment
self-documenting for audits and public questions.

The strongest framing is not "blockchain transparency" and not "crypto is
cheaper." The strongest framing is:

> CivicChain changes when control and evidence happen: before and during
> contract spending, not weeks later during reconciliation, audit prep, or
> public-record requests.

That creates five stakeholder wins:

| Audience | Primary win | Business effect | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Town manager | Political cover and time back | The office can answer "where did the money go?" from the live record instead of rebuilding it under pressure. | **Actual** as product story; **Target** as live outcome. |
| Municipality / city | Lower all-in vendor-payment cost | Payment certainty, record automation, spend control, and audit evidence reduce the expensive workflow around each contract. | **Modeled** until pilot data; use [[_docs/business/3-model/customer-savings-dollar-model]] for numbers. |
| Residents / public | Spending visible without a request | The town gets fewer staff lookup cycles and a more defensible answer when spending is challenged. | **Actual** as product story; full dashboard is **Target**. |
| Vendors / contractors | Faster, clearer payment path | The town can widen the bidder pool and lower modeled payment-risk premium by making approved work easier to finance. | **Modeled / Needs proof** until bid and payment-cycle data exists. |
| Workers / drivers | Cleaner field-spend workflow | The town/vendor get clearer spend control and fewer reimbursement/receipt disputes; personal worker convenience remains less proven. | **Open founder dependency**. |

Default planning economics from the dollar model: **$7K saved per $100K
contract, $25K/year for a tiny town, $115K/year for a mid municipality, and
$350K/year for a city/county vendor-spend book.**

## Town Manager

The town manager is the buyer and daily operator. The personal benefit is
**relief with control**: fewer defensive explanations, fewer Friday record
scrambles, and less chance that normal vendor work turns into a political
problem.

What changes:

- One console replaces email threads, spreadsheets, paper checks, manual status
  chasing, and "who has the latest version?" work. **Actual** as product story;
  **Target** as live deployment.
- The manager can point residents, reporters, auditors, and board members to the
  same public record instead of becoming the human search engine for every money
  question. **Actual** as product story; **Target** as full dashboard.
- Audit and public-record evidence assembles as work happens, so audit prep
  becomes a review of the record instead of a reconstruction project. **Modeled /
  Target**.
- Controlled spend can catch out-of-scope purchases before settlement instead of
  turning them into a year-end exception. **Target** while v1.0 card scope is
  unresolved.
- Vendor payment speed stays **inside the board / warrant workflow**: faster
  board-gated payment, not bypassing local authority.

Research support:

- Maine small-town finance is often a one-person, multi-role office.
- Maine vendor payments are structurally slow because treasurers disburse on a
  select-board warrant.
- Auditor shortage and FOAA burden make "audit-ready by Friday" and
  self-service records commercially meaningful.

## Municipality / City

The municipality wins at the operating level: taxpayer money becomes easier to
control, reconcile, audit, and explain. This is the economic buyer benefit.

What changes:

- Vendor risk premium falls in the model when payment timing and approved
  work-expense funding become more predictable. **Modeled / Needs proof**.
- Staff time falls in the model when contract records, payment authorization,
  spend events, and audit exports are already connected. **Modeled**.
- Out-of-scope spend is blocked or surfaced earlier through contract-bound
  controls. **Target**.
- Public-record and audit cost falls when residents, auditors, and staff look at
  the same evidence trail. **Modeled / Target**.
- The dollar model gives this benefit a usable default: **$25K/year saved for a
  tiny town, $115K/year for a mid municipality, $350K/year for a city/county
  book**. **Modeled / Bold internal scenario**.

Do not reduce this to "crypto avoids bank fees." The right unit is the all-in
cost per reconciled, audit-ready, legally valid vendor transaction.

## Residents / Public

Residents win because public money becomes inspectable without asking
permission. Their benefit is confidence, access, and a shorter path from
"what happened?" to "here is the record."

What changes:

- A resident can see active contracts, vendors, transactions, and verification
  hashes without filing a request, logging in, or calling town hall. **Actual**
  as product story.
- The buyer payoff is reduced political risk and fewer public-record lookup
  cycles: the town can point to the same record residents see. **Modeled /
  Target**.
- The full live public spend dashboard is **Target** and depends on product
  scope, card scope, and settlement mode.
- Resident payment fees are useful empathy context: the public already
  understands that government payment can feel slow and expensive. The core
  CivicChain comparison remains municipal vendor contract workflow, not a
  resident paying taxes, DMV fees, or utilities.
- The canon currently has a first-visit reason, not a proven recurring-use loop.
  Do not promise an engaged returning civic community without user evidence.

## Vendors / Contractors

Vendors win when CivicChain reduces uncertainty around approved work. A cleaner
payment path is not vendor charity; it is a buyer-side cost lever because it can
change how contractors price the job.

What changes:

- The vendor can see clearer contract status, allowed spend, remaining budget,
  and payment state. **Target**.
- Approved work-expense funding may become available earlier through a
  contract-bound pool. **Target**, gated by card scope.
- Faster board-gated payment reduces the working-capital wait vendors price into
  bids in the model. **Modeled / Needs proof**.
- Structured proposals can make bids easier to compare and defend. **Target**.

Strong wording: vendors stop financing as much municipal uncertainty, which can
widen the bidder pool and lower the modeled risk premium. Lines that break the
model: "same-day guaranteed payment" or "lower bids automatically save the town
by statute."

## Workers / Drivers

Workers are the least proven audience, but the benefit can be framed more
positively than before. Today the confirmed product value mainly gives the town
and vendor more control over field spend: named card, MCC lock, per-tap logging,
biometric wallet, approval/decline trail.

The likely worker benefit is practical: no personal cash at the pump, fewer
receipt hunts, fewer reimbursement delays, and clearer "this purchase is
allowed" rules. That is a strong adoption story, but it is not yet confirmed in
canon. Keep it as an open founder dependency until evidence exists.
